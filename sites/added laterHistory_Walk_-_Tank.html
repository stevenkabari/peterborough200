Water Scheme and Tank

Since the towns beginnings in 1876 the lack of permanent water and storage was an issue that had been continually debated. When the settlers arrived, they found the government had sunk two wells in the district. The “Nelia Well” on Mr H. Pomery’s property three miles west, and the “Jimmy’s Well” on Mr McInerney’s property, three miles to the east had been previously sunk on pastoral runs.

Mr Rohde enlisted help from Koch Brothers to sink a well on his property in 1876 and the following year one was sunk on Koch’s property.

On Tuesday 15th February 1881 a meeting was held at the Petersburg Hotel in regard to the “wants of the district.” It was decided to petition the government to establish a local court and asked them to assist in securing an adequate water supply for the town.

Due to the dry summer of 1881/1882 water carting for domestic and farming use from the public wells in the district was needed and farmers were forced to drive stock to water. The railway dam built in the town held little to no water and all other dams were dry.

In December 1885 Koch Brothers adopted a system for supplying a portion of the community. They erected a windmill on their well in the township which pumped water into two galvanised tanks at an elevation of about 10 feet. From these tanks pipes were laid and water was supplied to about a dozen families at a modest charge.

When it did rain there was no provision for water storage and other than fill domestic tanks most of the water soon dried up.

Water carting was again necessary in 1891 and as the population grew so did the urgency for a permanent water supply.

At a meeting on Friday 22nd April 1892 to discuss the subject of a permanent water supply, several schemes were suggested, the most popular being the sources of water from the Nelia Well. A committee was appointed and correspondence was sent to the government. They were advised by the members for the district that when the government were arranging for a supply for railway purposes would also consider the question of a supply for the town.

Express and Telegraph
Thursday 14 July 1892

A party of councillors and Mr Stewart, the engineer in charge of the selection of a site for a reservoir, visited a site for inspection on Thursday 20th April 1893. The reservoir which was to be for railway and town usage was to be about a mile almost due south of the Minvalara Dam, in the Hundred of Morgan and about twelve miles northeast of Petersburg. Mr Stewart stated that after examining the whole of the country within 12 miles of Petersburg, it appeared that the site now selected was the best suited for a water supply to Petersburg. The catchment area included about six square miles of country and the water was calculated to flow freely into the reservoir valley. The site was about seventy feet above the height of the town of Petersburg and therefore the water should gravitate from the supply to the town, and a pumping station and supply reservoir would be unnecessary.

The site was visited several weeks later by the Engineer-in-Chief (Mr A.B. Moncrieff) and he expressed to the mayor that he was well pleased with the site he had inspected and although no final decision had been made, he expected to have everything complete and the plans and details ready to be submitted to the government in about 3 weeks. He wished the local authorities to let him know in which streets they would like the mains laid down.

Another meeting was held to consider a permanent water supply to the town on Wednesday 21st February 1894 as the promised supply by the railways for the town and their usage had fallen through.

It wasn’t until August 1903 that a move was made by the government to establish a water supply in Petersburg. Several sites were visited with the purpose of testing for the suitability of establishing a reservoir. Mr Crowley’s farm about 2 miles from the township was the first site visited, followed by Paradise Alley, then the artesian bore at the Morgan Well on the Dawson Road, constructed years ago by Mr D. McKeough, Mr Donaldsons west of the town, the Gumbowie Ranges near Thyer’s Corner, and the racecourse.

In 1910 Petersburg was visited by C.A. Bayer and Mr Hicks (government hydraulic engineers) and they and the mayor Mr W. Butterworth visited the various sites suggested for the construction of a reservoir. They concluded that a reservoir would not be suitable and that the best source of water for the town would come from a well.

In June 1914 Mr Pink had success with the sinking of a well in Main Street and the council were approached by Mr Alexander (of the South Australian Well Drilling Company) and Mr Pink in reference to a council well. A well was sunk near the Power Station with good results.

The Council had been looking at an underground supply and in 1918 the Government sent an expert to test the Council well in the town. Tests were run and they showed that there was an ample supply of water and could be raised at a low cost. The water was tasted and it was exceptionally good. The water was then analysed and it was a “Knock-out blow” to find that it was saline, and not suitable for everyday use.

Petersburg Times
Friday 10 May 1918

This was a disappointment for the Council and the town and they felt that the report should not have been published in the press. It created a scare amongst the citizens who had been relying on wells for their water supply. Members of the Council were “dumbfounded” when they read the report and considered that they had been treated in an indiscreet manner and that it was an unwarranted de-framing of the town. The analysis was restudied and the water was declared fit to drink.

In 1924 a scheme to pump water from the Corporation Well by electric motor to an elevated tank and then pumped back to the town was considered.

In 1925 the residents of Peterborough finally gave permission by way of a petition for the go ahead of the scheme.

Testing in April 1926 saw a satisfactory result with the well pumped for 72 continuous hours. During that period 3,960 gallons per hour was pumped and a further test a few days later was also successful. The Commissioner of Public Works informed the council that his department would take over the running of the powerhouse well and that the water would be filtered with the use of a mechanical filtration plant.

Times and Northern Advertiser
Friday 31 October 1924

Pump on well at back of Power station - 1926
Photo – Peterborough Library History Room

In the assembly on Wednesday 24th November 1926, the Commissioner of Public Works announced that the Government had approved the purchase of the corporation well and the reserve on which it was situated, and £10,000 had been placed in the Loan Estimates for the Peterborough Water Scheme.

The scheme commenced in February 1927, with to government purchasing the site next to the power station which became known as the Engineering and Water Supply depot. Most houses in the town had their own supply of rainwater for human consumption and the water was mainly used for gardens and washing.

Times and Northern Advertiser
Friday 28 January 1927

After many delays and mishaps by August 1927 the motor had been erected at the powerhouse well and water had been pumped to the site of the water tank.

Building of the storage tank at the top of Government Road commenced on 23 August 1927 after the instillation of the motor at the well site. A small gang of men had been engaged to lay the pipe main along Victoria Street and had progressed as far as Jervois Street and was almost completed.

Water storage tank under construction - 1927
Photo – Peterborough Library History Room

At a council meeting on Monday 5th March 1928, Mr Green, the Waterworks Engineer informed the meeting that the tank was almost complete and could hold 10 feet of water at present. Arrangements had been made to supply electric current to the tank along Government Road. This also meant that about 20 houses could be served from the same line. The Council erected the line at a cost of 30/- per pole for the government. Mr Green also advised the council that the water would be supplied to the Town Hall free of charge for fire purposes only.

The tank could hold half a million gallons of water and at last the town had a good supply of water with good pressure of 90 bs to the square inch.

With a plentiful supply of water, the show committee was finally able to improve the showgrounds and there was a call to establish a bowling green as previous attempts had failed due to the lack of water. Although the town now had water on supply, homes were equipped with rain water tanks and most people relied on tank water to drink and cook in.

The Fire Brigade Board inspected Peterborough and were satisfied that an auxiliary fire brigade could be formed as soon as the council forwarded the necessary application forms. The council would need to provide a fire station, at an estimated cost of £850, which the board would pay a rental of 8 per cent per annum.

There were concerns raised by the Water Works department in January 1930 that due to the severe strain of continual pumping for 18 hours a day during the past 18 months, along with the continued dry seasons, the supply was in danger.

The government announced water restrictions at Peterborough as there were too many consumers for the water available and pumping was reduced. The daily supply was cut from 240,000 gallons to 70,000 gallons, and they appealed to residents to cease garden watering and to exercise general care when using water.

The scheme that was put into operation in 1928 was designed for 250 houses but by December 1930 there were over 500 connected to the scheme. Residents were paying water rates even though the water was not available to water lawns and flower gardens.

A new sanitary system was suggested in August 1932, which would require about 28,000 gallons of water per day. The conversion from pan to deep drainage system was unfortunately not able to be recommended as the water scheme could not handle the extra need for water.

The water shortage was again discussed at length at a February council meeting, and it was resolved to approach the government on the question of supplementing the scheme. By May the members of the district were to be approached to ascertain the result of the communication regarding the inadequate water supply in Peterborough. The government purchased two wells in section 209 from Mr Price and Mr Lloyd to supplement the water supply, although this didn’t seem to help matters.

BY 1938 the water situation had not been resolved and residents and council were still not able to water their gardens. The council wrote to the Engineering and water supply for permission to use sprinklers on the lawns in the Main Street. It was suggested that the council take over the water scheme from the government.

Times and Northern Advertiser
Friday 25 February 1938

The Business Men’s Progress Society had been pushing for water to be supplied from the Murray River. The matter was bought before the Public Works Standing Committee in May 1939. The town council were in favour, but the district councillors felt that landowners could not afford the rate that would be imposed under the scheme.

Times and Northern Advertiser
Friday 12 May 1939

Continued dry conditions in 1940 saw most residents Peterborough out of rainwater and forced to drink and cook in the town water supply, which was considered unfit for human consumption by many people. The local board of health suggested that the council contact the government with an endeavour to improve the quality of the water now that in was being used in place of rainwater. A sample was forwarded to the central board of health to ascertain whether the water was suitable for drinking purposes. It was sent to the Government Department of Chemistry and a detailed analysis found increased salt, but a decreased nitrate content from a previous analysis in 1938. The water was found to be organically sound.

A committee was formed in 1945 to stand behind the proposition to improve the present water supply and have the Murray River bought to Peterborough.

Times and Northern Advertiser
Friday 8 December 1944

More water restrictions were imposed in February 1945 as the level of the well had fallen. The restrictions were lifted in April.

The Public Standing Committee along with the district engineer of the E&WS department visited Peterborough in May 1947 with regard with providing the town with an improved and adequate water supply. It was remarked that the committee had visited in 1939, but the war had interrupted the review of evidence, and the committee had resumed its enquiries.

Times and Northern Advertiser
Friday 15 May 1947

A plan to provide water from the Morgan-Whyalla pipe line to Jamestown, Caltowie and country to the west was approved on Monday 12th January 1948. This plan also included the future inclusion of Peterborough and Terowie when an extension could be considered.

Tenders were called for the construction of a million-gallon water storage tank for Peterborough and the SA Minister of Works approved the necessary expenditure to investigate a sewerage scheme.

Due to the shortage of manpower and materials there was no response to the tender. The chief storekeeper had placed an order for sufficient steel to be imported but as an import licence had not been granted, it was not certain that the government could obtain the needed material.

At a dinner at Casey’s hotel held by the Mayor Mr E.H. Fowler on Monday 26th September 1949, the local representative Mr M.R. O’Halloran MP, spoke about the town being connected to the Murray River water scheme. The construction of the new million-gallon storage tank was due to start, and the steel reinforcing rods had arrived.

Times and Northern Advertiser
Friday 30 September 1949

A public meeting on Friday 4th December 1953 saw over 500 people resolve a resolution to urge the government to extend the water main being laid at Jamestown to Peterborough. The water pressure in the town had been so low that water was only available for a limited time each day. The hospital was without water of half the day, and the fire hazard was high due to the low pressure of water. The railway dam at Gumbowie was empty and water carting for the railways at Terowie was costing the government a fortune. This would be reduced if water was piped to Peterborough. The bore water the town relied on was so mineralised that it crumbled the stone of buildings. The local board of health had condemned the swimming pool, and it had to be disposed of.

Several new industries were wanting to be established in the town but cannot do so because of the quality and quantity of the water available.

Finally in December 1954, the government approved a proposal to extend a branch-main from the Morgan to Whyalla pipeline to provided Peterborough with a permanent water supply.

The Public Works Standing Committee took evidence in April 1955, and the town was finally connected with the Murray River - Morgan to Whyalla pipeline in January 1958.
