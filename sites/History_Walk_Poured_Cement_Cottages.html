100 Year History Walk

Poured Cement
Railway Cottages

In the mid 1920s Peterborough had a serious and growing housing shortage, so the railways built more than a hundred poured cement cottages at Hill View. This history walk explains why they were built, which railway families lived in them and how they were designed, with floor plans and historic photographs. Learn how a Baptist and a Methodist church, a shop, tennis courts and a playground grew around the new estate, and find familiar local family names in the 1930 electoral roll listing.

Housing had been a difficulty in the town since its inception. Workshops had been relocated from Port Pirie in 1891, and the influx of 60 workers caused a huge shortage of accommodation. Many families were crowded into shared boarding houses or lived on the outskirts of the town in tents. Other workers lived permanently in hotels.

The planned expansion of the Railways in Peterborough and relocation of the paint and repair shops from Quorn in 1925, exacerbated the shortage of housing in the town.

The matter was brought before parliament by Mr M.H. O’Halloran who asked the Chief Commissioner if he was aware that many railway employees were unable to obtain houses in Peterborough and was it the intention of the premier to consider the advisability of building a number of railway cottages there. The Premier the Hon J. Gunn replying to Mr O’Halloran in the Assembly, stated that the Railways Commissioner hoped to complete two hundred cottages for employees at Peterborough by June 30, 1926.

The Railways purchased an area known as Hill View owned by Mr Cochrane west of Peterborough. Housing lots were laid out with two hundred cottages to be built at a cost of £700 each. These cottages were made of poured cement and over 200 workers were employed to build them.

By 28th February 1926, twelve of these cottages were ready. The workers relocated from Quorn were given preference for these cottages. Forty-five more were completed by September 1926 with a further thirty more waiting for tanks, wire doors and fencing. Railway workers were charged £1 per week for rent with 6d for water and 9d for use of electric lights. Power meters were installed in 1936. These cottages had five rooms with conveniences such as bathrooms, wash troughs and coppers connected with rain water tanks. They were also provided with septic tanks, water and electric lights. By September seventy-five of the poured cement houses were built on the Hill View Estate with a further thirty-five completed by the end of 1926.

Although it was proposed to build two hundred of these cottages, only one hundred and ten were actually built. The remaining cottages were to be built south of Buxton Crescent, but this never eventuated.

Out of all the cottages built there are three different floorplans although there were several different shapes and frontages. Most of the cottages built in Railway Terrace, Queen Street, Wright Street, and Princess Street are of a square shape with either a flat roofed front porch or a gabled porch, although some houses have the porch on the side. The cottages in Buxton Crescent and some in Wright Street are a longer shape with stone pillars on their porches. The cottages in Buxton Crescent have more ornate thinner pillars. In the longer shaped houses the third bedroom is accessible through the lounge room.

In 1926 a red brick church was built by the Baptists in Hill View along the new road (Queen Street which was just a dirt track) to the Railway cottages to service the residents. The church was opened on Sunday 3rd April 1927 and services continued until it closed around 1951.

A Methodist Church was built in Hill Street and opened in 1927 with an addition of a hall to be used as a Sunday school. This building was made of wood and iron. In 1952 the brick church built by the Baptist Church was purchased by the Methodist Church.

In 1952 the old iron Methodist Church and Hall were moved from its Hill Street site adjacent to the church in Queen Street on the Cyanide Street side. The church was pushed by railwaymen, church members and the New Australian workforce on a specially constructed railway line, and was done over two weekends. This was used as the Sunday School. The church was reopened on Saturday 16th August 1952. A kitchen was added to the Hall in 1955. The original iron church was sold in 1969 and removed. The Peterborough West Methodist Church was closed on 29th May 1976 and was sold in 1984.

A committee was formed and the residents of the new railway cottages held a fete on 19th December 1927 to raise money for swings and equipment for the children’s playground.

A shop was opened in Queen Street around 1927 by Mr Sexton and the tennis courts were opened in February 1928.
