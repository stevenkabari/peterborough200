100 Year History Walk

Maternity Wing of
Peterborough Soldiers
Memorial Hospital

Peterborough spent more than thirty determined years fighting for a hospital of its own. This history walk follows the fundraising, the setbacks and the community spirit that led to the opening of the Soldiers Memorial Hospital in 1922, and the Mother’s Wing that followed in 1926. Discover the early private nurses and doctors, the carnivals, dances and fetes that raised the money, and the many nurses who trained here. Original newspaper clippings and photographs show how the whole town built its hospital together.

A meeting was held on Friday 27th February 1891 to consider the advisability of providing hospital accommodation in the town of Petersburg. A further meeting was held on Monday 25th April 1892. As a result of that meeting a Hospital Fund was established and the residents began fundraising. In 1895 the hospital was closed at Waukaringa and it was hoped that the government would remove the weatherboard and iron building to Petersburg.

A meeting of subscribers to the Petersburg Hospital Fund was held on Friday 4th February 1898 and the mayor conveyed to the small gathering that correspondence from the Chief Secretary informed them that the government could not help the town in any way to erect a hospital.

Petersburg Times
Friday 11 February 1898

In July 1899 Victoria Jubilee Park was purchased by the council from the trustee Mr Rohde with the money from the Hospital Funds. The residents were led to believe that a hospital would be erected at the site of the park.

Petersburg Times
Friday 9th June 1899

Some private residents operated as ‘hospitals’ over the years including Mrs Holland (Brady), Nurse Quinn and Nurse Lowry at 83 Victoria Street. She moved to the corner of Collins and Bourke Street in 1919. Several ran as a maternity hospital run by a midwife, one being located at 4 High Street. The state school was used several times as a temporary hospital when there had been an outbreak of influenza or other disease in the town.

Resident doctors offered consulting rooms at their premises. Dr Clarke consulted in Bismark Street, two doors west from the Baptist Church in 1912, before building his house and consulting rooms in Jervois Street in 1919. After the tragic death of Dr Clarke in 1922, Dr Schafer ran his practice from the Jervois Street premises, followed by Dr Morris in 1930, Dr Myers in 1944, Dr Webster in March 1958, Dr Bielby in September 1958 and Dr Shortt in 1966. Veterinarian Dr Tonuma purchased the property in December 1968.

A meeting was held on Wednesday 1st February 1911, convened by the mayor William Butterworth to urge upon the government the necessity of building a hospital in the town. The meeting was attended by a large number of ratepayers and the majority of those present felt that it was “highly desirable that a hospital should be built for the district.” - Petersburg Times – Friday 3 February 1911.

In 1913 the committee under the chairmanship of Dr C. Lowther Clarke obtained a promise of assistance from the government and fundraising began.

It was proposed at a meeting in May 1914 “to raise a sum of £2,000 by public conscription, to which will be added the government subsidy of £3,000 and thus a total of £5,000 will be available for building and furnishing a hospital. The plans provided for a hospital of 20 beds.” – Daily Herald – Tuesday 19 May 1914.

The fundraising was eased with the outbreak of war although £500 had been raised by donations and social occasions.

Following the end of the war the desire to establish a hospital in Peterborough was rejuvenated and a committee headed by Dr Arthur Goode was elected in 1919. It was decided to build the hospital as a lasting and worthy memorial to our brave men who had fought in the war.

SA Chief Secretary JG Bice visited the town to open a fundraising fair for the hospital. Mr Bice was met at the station by Mayor SD Jones and Councillor SW Dickson and driven to the hospital site on six acres donated by Mr J Cochrane.

Tenders were called for the construction of the hospital and were due on Monday 1st March 1920. The plans and specifications had been completed and the hospital would be the most up to date in the state.

Mr Haese of Jamestown was awarded the contract with his tender of £12,000.

The foundations for the Peterborough Soldiers Memorial Hospital had been laid on 20th August 1920 and the nurses quarters, infectious wards and kitchen block were almost complete. The foundation stone was laid by the Governor Sir Archibald Weigall on Tuesday 9th September. £4,000 had been raised but in order to open the hospital free of debt at least £2,000 more was required that year.

By August 1921 work was progressing and the grounds were coming along.

The Times
Friday 12 August 1921

The opening of the hospital was unduly delayed and a further £1,000 was needed to open the hospital free of debt.

The Times
Friday 27 January 1922

The opening of the Peterborough Soldiers Memorial Hospital was held on Wednesday 5th April by the Chief Secretary and Acting Premier (the Hon J.G. Bice) in inclement weather. The school children assembled outside the gateway and marched to the lawn and the Returned Soldiers assembled under the main entrance steps. The opening was performed by the Chief Secretary and Acting Premier (The Hon. J. G. Bice). The hospital was then thrown open for public inspection, guided by staff. Afternoon tea was then served on the grounds. A carnival was held in the Town Hall in the evening and was a great success with a further £450 raised.

The hospital opened completely debt free and was the most up to date in the state. The people of Peterborough were to be congratulated for the fundraising effort they put in to build the hospital.

Peterborough Soldiers Memorial Hospital
Photo – Lionel Noble Collection

By 28th April there were 18 patients under treatment at the hospital. The residents of Peterborough continued to support the hospital with many giving a variety of donations including meat (rabbits, chickens and sheep), cream and butter, cakes, vegetables, eggs, sauce, pickles, chutneys, flowers, wood, magazines, papers, books, clothes and linen.

Dr Clarke had plans to increase the accommodation for the nursing staff and following his tragic death in September 1922 the hospital board continued with fundraising for the additions.

A large gathering assembled at the hospital grounds on Saturday 24th November 1923 for the opening of the new nurses quarters in memory of the late C. Lowther Clarke, and unveiling of the memorial plaque. The building had been completed at a cost of £1,150 of which £150 remained to be raised. The new building was situated behind the other nurses’ quarters at the rear of the hospital and consisted of six rooms, passage right through both ways and surrounded by wide verandas, which may be used for sleeping out.

In April 1925 the returned soldiers club sold its premises as members could no longer support its overdraft and the guarantor wished to be free of liability. A vote of 23 to five confirmed the club’s closure and sale proceeds were donated to the hospital in return for which veterans were given free treatment.

There was a great need for a maternity ward at the hospital. There was no baby welfare centre here and maternity cases could not be taken in the general hospital on account of lack of space and the noise, which would not be beneficial to other patients. Children under the age of two were not admitted to the hospital for the same reason. “The town was served by 3 or 4 well kept homes, providing accommodation for about 16 patients, but even these were not certain of continuing, as the nurses were anxious to close their homes.” The Times – Friday 22 May 1925.

The residents of Peterborough again started fundraising with many events held to raise money. The Nelia orchestra held a dance in the town hall, a Queen competition was held raising £887 and the hospital fete raised over £1,000.

Despite the wet and cold a large crowd assembled at the Hospital Grounds on Friday 21st August 1925 to witness Mrs John Gunn, wife of the Premier, lay the foundation stone for the new maternity wing. Mr Alex Jamieson, (Chairman of the Hospital Board) presented Mrs Gunn with a silver trowel in honour of laying the stone.

The Maternity Block of the Peterborough Hospital was opened on Friday 12th February 1926 by the Premier (Hon John Gunn).

After an official inspection the premier (Mr Gunn), chairman of the board (Mr Alex Jamieson), the mayor (Mr William Reed), invited guests, members of the hospital board, councillors and hospital staff assembled on the front veranda for the opening. The school children and fife band marched to the hospital and sang ‘The song of Australia’ before Mr Jamieson welcomed the visitors. The maternity wing was named the “Mother’s Wing” and he felt that no better name could have been chosen.

Mr Gunn congratulated the residents of Peterborough on their very fine achievement in erecting the maternity wing. The people of the town and district had raised £5,600 including £3,000 from the proceeds of entertainment. The government had contributed a subsidy for a total of £16,000.

The premier unlocked the door and declared the maternity block open. The visitors were entertained at afternoon tea by the hospital board.

A commemorative banquet was held that night by the mayor (Mr William Reed) at the Railway Hotel in connection with the opening, followed by a music programme.

The Chronicle
Saturday 20 February 1926

In early 1926 the maternity block was registered as a training school in midwifery and many hundreds of training midwifery and general nurses trained at the hospital throughout the following years. By 1928 no probationary nurse or midwifery trainee was engaged unless educational requirements of the South Australian Nurses Registration Board had been met and certificates had been produced. They also had to be approved by a committee.

In August 1930 the Nurses Registration Board requested that 75 percent of nurses employed at the Peterborough Hospital were partly-trained nurses. The Hospital Board were willing to engage 25 percent of partly trained nurses.

In 1949 the hospital was in desperate need of trained nurses and there was a thought that the hospital would have to be closed.

The Times
Friday 16 May 1954

The Times
Friday 14 January 1949

The unsatisfactory conditions of the nurses and domestic quarters were discussed in 1945. They were short staffed and advertised for four domestics to relieve the arduous duties of the nurses.

A public meeting was called on 16th June 1927 by the mayoress Mrs R.W. Goudie at the invitation of the board of management of the Peterborough Hospital, with the object of starting a Peterborough and District woman’s auxiliary. The object of the auxiliary would be ‘to assist in the welfare of necessitous cases among patients, to co-operate and assist the board of management of Peterborough Hospital in repair and upkeep of hospital linen, and to render aid in any other welfare matter they may deem necessary.’ – The News – Monday 13 June 1927.

The Times
Friday 24 June 1927

The Peterborough and Districts Citizens’ Hospital Insurance Fund was launched in January 1927. The fund was similar to the Railway Workers Hospital Bed Fund and covered members for hospital treatment and expenses during illness, with special consideration for families. The fund began operation on 1st July with Mr W.H. Bennett appointed chairman and Mr M.E. Cope secretary.

By October 1927 the daily patient average for the hospital was 20 and the maternity hospital four.

The Peterborough and Districts Citizen’s Hospital Insurance Bed Fund joined the SA Citizen’s Bed Fund in 1931 which offered cover for hospital expenses anywhere in the state.
